When the Prior Steps

A frozen humanoid motion prior will step over an obstacle on request. It will not step over your obstacle. We measured why: the prior emits the requested behaviour once, early — median frame 34 of 200 — and no conditioning channel we tried moves that timing. The practical consequence is that placement is won by staging the scene around the prior, not by commanding the prior, and that costs 7–8 generator calls.

Finding

One motion, two obstacles

These two panels play the same generated clip, frame for frame. Nothing about the motion differs. Only the obstacle moved — from where the prior actually steps, to the mid-route position a scene would specify. That single change is the difference between clearing the box and walking into it.

It is non-zero only where a foot leaves the ground, and the dashed marker is where the scene asked for that lift.

The prior is not unable to step over. It is unable to be told where.
Context

Why this matters for humanoid research

Large motion priors are increasingly proposed as the motion substrate for humanoid systems: a frozen generator supplies natural whole-body movement, a tracker executes it, and a planner is supposed to steer the pair through a scene. That division of labour assumes the planner can address the generator — that asking for a behaviour at a place and time produces that behaviour at that place and time.

We tested that assumption exhaustively for one behaviour, stepping over an obstacle, through every conditioning channel the released interface exposes. It does not hold, and the reason is specific and reusable: the timing of an elicited behaviour is a property of the generator's own rollout, not of the conditioning. A planner that issues spatial commands to such a prior is writing into a channel the decoder does not read.

The finding is worth reporting because the failure is invisible in the metrics the field commonly uses. Every arm we ran looks like a step-over: the behaviour is present, the motion is natural, a foot leaves the ground. Scored on foot height or on semantic similarity to a prompt, all of it passes. Scored on whether the robot clears the box that was actually in front of it, almost none of it does.

Result 2

Result 3

The mechanism: once, early

Sixty-four clips under the prompt alone, on one fixed route, with every lift located in space and converted to its frame in the clip. The distribution is the explanation for every negative above.

Median frame 34 (1.36 s), 10th–90th percentile 20–54, and 80 % of all lifts inside the first 50 frames. Restricting to lifts above 8 cm does not move it (median frame 33). Only 3 of 49 land after frame 60 — the prior expresses the prompt shortly after its context settles, then returns to walking.

What this explains Every earlier negative in this program follows from one sentence: the event's when is set by the rollout, so no channel specifying where can move it. A pilot that waited for a gait event at a predeclared obstacle found one eligible seed in eight. A pilot that warped route timing to bring the root to the obstacle kept the gait in one case of six. And an earlier text experiment that reported "elicits but places randomly" was never seeing randomness — it was seeing early, against obstacles that were elsewhere.

The prior is not uniformly unaddressable — it has a narrow window near 1.0–1.4 m of route and is exactly zero beyond 3.4 m. Averaging over obstacle positions spread across the whole route, as a naive evaluation would, hides the window and reports a uniformly hopeless prior.

Method

Stage the scene, then select

If the prior cannot be told when to act, the planner's job changes from commanding to arranging. Measure when the prior acts, place the robot so that moment lands on the obstacle, and spend samples to cover the residual variance.

  1. Stage the approach. Walk the robot to a point roughly 1.2 m (≈ 1.36 s) before the obstacle, and begin a fresh generation there. This is not a coordinate shift of an existing clip — the prior is not translation-equivariant, and offsetting a route is absorbed as a speed change. The offset must come from re-planning the approach.
  2. Generate under the prompt alone. No packet, no rotation channel, no root-height channel — the arm that beat every structured alternative.
  3. Measure, don't trust. Score whole-body box clearance at the obstacle against the simulation collision model. Accept or resample.
  4. Budget 7–8 calls. At the staged optimum, a single sample clears a 5 cm box with probability 0.312 and an 8 cm box with 0.266, so eight calls reach 95 % and 92 % respectively.

Honest naming This is selection, not control, and we do not intend to dress it as a controller. Its scientific content is the cost curve and the staging geometry that makes the curve affordable — both of which are measurements of the prior, reusable by anyone building on a frozen generator.

Primary endpoint

Obstacle-centred whole-body box clearance — the tallest box the entire body clears at the obstacle, against the robot's simulation collision model with a separately measured margin. Never swing-foot peak, which passes for motions that collide.

Supporting vector

Collision-free flag, crossing position and frame error, lead-side match, stance and contact fractions, foot-floor penetration, progress ratio, integrated program deformation, generator calls and wall-clock.

Inference unit

Scene, never the seed. Seeds are the resampling budget within a scene. Wilson intervals on every rate; descriptive paired differences with no interval claimed at these sample sizes.

Controls that caught errors

A free nominal arm, an outcome-free constructibility probe, and a byte-identical determinism replay. Each caught an invalid comparison before it produced a claim.

Scope

What this does not show

One prior, one behaviour, one geometry. Everything here is ARDY-G1 with a released checkpoint, a step-over, and a 20 cm-deep box on a straight route. Duck and lateral squeeze have their own contracts and are untested under this analysis. Cross-prior replication on Kimodo-G1 is staged but unrun.

No execution claim. Every number is kinematic. The operating envelope the method targets, 5–8 cm, is precisely the amplitude at which whole-body tracking previously consumed most of the clearance margin, so the tracker must enter the endpoint before any of this is called a traversal. The SONIC path is verified callable and is the first item in the next campaign.

The timing window is a measurement, not a theory. Frame 34 is a property of this prior at this history and horizon configuration. Whether the window is set by the autoregressive context length, by the text-conditioning schedule, or by the training distribution is untested, and it is the most interesting open question the result raises.

Staging assumes a run-up. A 1.2 m approach is available in a corridor with one obstacle and not in dense clutter. The honest generalisation is receding-horizon replanning at each obstacle, where the per-clip rate becomes the cost driver and the budget compounds.

Proposal

What we would run next

The next campaign is drafted and its budget is already measured. It is deliberately not a controller: the evidence says a controller has nothing to act on.

Staged selection, with the tracker in the loop

Five arms — one-shot, staged selection at N=8, an unstaged control that isolates staging from resampling, a packet arm at the same budget, and the walk floor. Accepted clips replayed through SONIC and scored on achieved state. Graded box heights 0.03–0.30 m rather than pass/fail on one demanding obstacle.

What would change our mind

If the staged rate survives tracking, the method ships and the paper is about measuring a generator's addressable window. If tracking eats the 5–8 cm envelope, the honest result is a cost curve for frozen priors as planners — and the line ends there rather than in another controller.

The open mechanism question

Why frame 34? Varying history length, generation horizon, and prompt schedule would establish whether the window is a tunable of the interface or a property of autoregressive motion priors in general. The latter would matter well beyond this system.

Deliberately deferred

The output-space adapter — authoring the event outside the prior and stitching it in — stays last, and must first be beaten by a procedural, non-learned stitch. Our own packet failed precisely by pulling motion off the prior's manifold, which is that approach's characteristic risk, so its judge must be the tracker.
