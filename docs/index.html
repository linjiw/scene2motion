Scene2Motion · research project page · updated 2026-09-02

Can a humanoid produce the right motion, at the right place, and still clear the obstacle when a controller follows it?

A humanoid can generate a stepping motion without stepping over the box in front of it. Scene2Motion studies this gap between motion generation and obstacle traversal. Using a pretrained motion model (NVIDIA ARDY-G1) and an existing controller (SONIC) on the Unitree G1, we test where motions occur, whether they clear the scene, and whether clearance survives tracking. We also use measured clearance errors to improve ducking motions and record why other candidates are rejected.

Placement

Can the motion be placed at the obstacle?

We asked the model for a step in every way its released interface allows: the text prompt, a foot-position request, a rotation request aligned to the gait, and a prompt switched on mid-walk. For each we counted, in order, whether the step was produced, whether the reference passes the support test, whether it clears a 5 cm box at the scene's fixed position, and whether that clearance survives tracking.

44 / 64step-prompted references lift a foot ≥ 3 cm somewhere on the route
12 / 64clear a 5 cm box at the fixed position (1.2 m; chosen after inspecting the clips, now fixed for replication)
3 / 16produce the step when the prompt is switched on mid-walk, 0.8–3.0 s late, none at the predicted position
  • The step comes early and on its own schedule. The leading foot crosses the route's midline at a median 1.4 s; 40 of 49 lifting clips lift inside the first two seconds. Where the box is does not move it.
  • Joint-level requests do not place it either. A requested foot lift over-responds by 1.7–2.3× and lifts both feet; a rotation request produces a step 3.2 m from its anchor at half the amplitude; a route stretched to line the step up with the box preserves the intended gait in 1 of 6.
  • Letting the box move to fit the clip is a different statement. Within ±0.25 m of the target, 20 of 64 clear; at the fixed position, 12. We report both, labelled.
Tracking

Does clearance survive when the controller follows the reference?

All 64 references were tracked by SONIC in Isaac Sim. The box was not in the physics scene; the recorded robot states were replayed against its geometry afterwards. None of the 12 clearing references preserved clearance after tracking. The controller's evaluator stopped 53 of the 64 rollouts on its tracking-error rule, and every stopped robot was still upright.

0 / 64references whose clearance is preserved after tracking
53 / 64rollouts stopped by the evaluator's tracking-error cutoff; 0 of 53 fell (pelvis 0.56–0.95 m at the last frame)
53 / 53 · 8 / 11the reference screen rejects every stopped reference and passes 8 of the 11 completed runs, rejecting three

Why: the step is not a supported step

A foot meets the support test when its sole is within 4.65 cm of the ground and moving slower than 1.18 m/s. In all 12 clearing references both feet fail that test for 0.44–1.04 s (nine clips) or 2.4–3.1 s (three) while the pelvis rises only 2–26 cm. That is not a jump either: the unsupported period is 1.3–15.6× longer than a ballistic flight for that rise. The evaluator's cutoff lands within ±0.2 s of the start of that period in 47 of 53 rollouts.

What the screen does and does not say. It predicts one evaluator's stopping rule under the release configuration. It does not say the robot would fall, and it does not say the motion is physically impossible. The runs without that stopping rule, and a known-trackable jumping control, are the next experiments.

Prospective test, in progress

128 fresh references (32 seeds under four root-control settings) were generated, scored, and the screen's per-clip predictions committed before any tracking run. The free setting replicates the original (23 of 32 produce the step; 7 of 32 clear at the fixed position). Tracking is queued.

Repair

Correcting measured clearance errors: ducking under beams

Ducking is where measurement can fix the reference. The model's root-height control moves overhead clearance in a predictable direction, so a measured error identifies a correction: Δq = e / |g′|, applied at most twice, with the reference remeasured after each step and rejected with its residual error if the response saturates. Stepping has no such control in our experiments and is not corrected.

What the table says. 36 multi-beam scenes × 8 seeds. For the learned proposer, two corrections raise the collision-free rate from 72.9 % to 99.3 %, against 77.4 % for resampling with the same three-attempt budget; in 63 scene-seed pairs only correction succeeds, in none only resampling. That 99.3 % is not a margin result: the 18 cm margin is met in 37.5 % of pairs. For the heuristic proposer, whose references are already almost always collision-free, resampling reaches a higher margin rate than correction. Measurement helps when the proposal has a systematic error and adds little when it does not.

These are reference-geometry results. An earlier tracking study of 526 ducking references preserved clearance in none, under a protocol whose 14 s clip cap stopped most rollouts before the beam; it says nothing about the corrections either way. The experiment that would, with the beam present in the physics scene, is listed under what comes next.

Records

Motion records with rejection reasons

Every candidate is kept, accepted or not, with its measurements and the reason it was rejected. A rejected candidate is a statement about that reference under this controller's evaluator; it does not mean the scene cannot be traversed.

What a record carries

Scene and obstacle; the request (prompt, seed, controls); where the motion occurred; clearance at the scene's position; the support-test result and the screen's prediction; correction steps and residual error; the evaluator outcome and cutoff time; clearance after tracking; physics seed; provenance for the model, runtime, robot model and controller.

Rejection labels

Reference intersects the obstacle · screen predicts an evaluator cutoff · tracking stops before reaching the obstacle · achieved motion clears the obstacle. Four separate labels, not one "untraversable".

Ducking pilot, 300 procedural scenes, one proposer, at most two corrections: 268 collision-free references (192 meeting the 18 cm margin, 76 below it), 6 rejected after correction with their residual error, 26 refused before correction with a reason. Stepping: 64 + 128 references, each with every label above. Whether these records improve a navigation policy is a separate experiment that has not been run.

What comes next

Three experiments, in order

1 · What the screen actually predicts

Track the 128 fresh references against the committed predictions. Run the original 64 without the tracking-error cutoff and classify the physical outcome: fell, stalled, continued, cleared. Add a known-trackable jumping example before reading an airborne phase as impossible.

2 · One convincing ducking experiment

A walking control that reaches the beam. Uncorrected, corrected and resampled references over predetermined beam heights, with rollouts long enough to reach the beam and the beam present in the physics scene. Does correcting the reference's clearance improve the robot's achieved clearance? Collisions, failing to reach the beam and evaluator cutoffs kept separate.

3 · Do the records help a policy?

Test whether records with rejection labels help a navigation policy decide whether to duck, step over or take another route. That decision is the longer-term goal and stays in the roadmap until the complete behaviour is demonstrated.

Experiment identifiers, protocols, seeds, schedules and the full history are in the research log and the plan of record.
