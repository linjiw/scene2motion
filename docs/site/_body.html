Which generated motions can a controller use to get through this obstacle?

A humanoid can perform a convincing step-over and still walk into the box, because the step happens somewhere else. This page plays the archived motions behind that claim and shows the measurements beside them: where the motion happens, whether it clears the obstacle the scene specifies, and what is left after a controller tracks it.

Latest research · September 5, 2026 · simulation + archived-data analysis

Local beam passage works. Motion quality is the next question.

Scene2Motion now has obstacle-present ducking results with frozen ARDY-G1 and SONIC. The strongest established setting here is a fixed three-second native duck, not a learned repair: 17 / 32 local passages in the default-mode A5 campaign. Changing the encoder mode does not improve that total. The stepping repair remains a negative result.

What the current method actually does

  1. Scene → native motion controls

    Specify the beam geometry, forward route and a smooth root-height schedule. The tested duck uses a 0.28 m requested dip and quintic entry/recovery ramps; fixed2 and fixed3 change the hold-end time, not the frozen model weights.

  2. Frozen prior → frozen controller

    ARDY generates 208 frames at 25 Hz from the cached WALK prompt and native constraints. Freeze every array, then track it in 32 isolated Isaac environments with SONIC. Paired absent/present arms share references, order and physics seed.

  3. Achieved motion → separate evidence labels

    Check actual beam placement, controller mode and achieved whole-body passage. Save failures, cutoffs, missing suffixes and recovery diagnostics alongside successes. Geometry, contact and motion quality remain different labels.

The proposed compiler is broader than the implemented result. It would qualify the final edited motion and choose a bounded scene-relative correction that preserves both clearance and progress. That improvement is not established yet. The earlier stepping backend used bounded per-frame nonlinear IK over twelve leg joints—not a support QP—and completed 0 / 2 repaired-present local crossings. Neither passing a support proxy nor reference clearance guarantees execution.

A5's operational local passage requires the rear body envelope beyond x = 1.32 m, root beyond x = 1.82 m, upright and inside the corridor for 0.20 s by 4.0 s, with no nominal or 40 mm margin-inflated geometric hazard in that prefix. The beam underside is 1.15 m. This is not a physical-contact-free endpoint or the separate 7.2 m full-route goal. Frozen protocol.

Contact logging and lower-memory execution

What contact validation establishes

Six corrected simple-body controls passed. Sixty-four G1 operational replays reproduce A5 achieved states, apparatus and modes exactly. The 32 present valid prefixes report zero Table contacts across 30 robot-body filters, but the untrimmed reader reached six points. Excluded-event attribution and a controlled G1 positive-contact probe remain open. Prefix zeros are not episode-level contact freedom.

What the resource optimization establishes

Separate preparation/validation workers and a small resident supervisor avoid keeping model inputs loaded beside Isaac. Compact allocation retained the same 32 slots and exact control replay outcomes. A5 used 18.54 minutes of simulator-process time and peaked at 5.96 GiB child RSS. This is measured memory use, not an end-to-end speedup or permission to lower resource gates.

From execution records to a useful dataset

The existing 300-record DB preview is geometry/feasibility metadata, not 300 executed demonstrations. A5 adds grouped execution records, but they are not yet a qualified imitation bank or integrated public motion release. Keep every carrier's raw/edited, absent/present, mode and repeat variants in one split. Contact unknown stays unknown.

  2. Freeze qualification and one bounded edit: require final-output progress and clearance; test on fresh motion–scene groups against fixed2/fixed3 and budget-disclosed controls.
  3. Then expand data claims: scene-disjoint coverage, payload redistribution review and a pre-execution learner utility test. No broad navigation, hardware or safety claim yet.

The problem

One motion, two obstacles

These two panels play the same generated clip, frame for frame. Nothing about the motion differs. Only the box moves — from where the model happened to step, to the mid-route position a scene would specify. That is the difference between clearing the box and walking into it.

Side view at the apex of the highest swing; press play to run the motion. Fading traces follow the feet, the swing side in green. The strip beneath is the clearance profile over the whole route: for every position, the tallest box the entire body would clear there, measured against the robot's own collision primitives with a 4 cm margin. The dashed marker is where the scene asked for the clearance. In the left panel the box was placed after watching the clip, so it shows capability, not placement — it is not a traversal result.

Producing a stepping motion does not make it happen at the box.

Local traversal means passing through the specified corridor; walking around does not count. Navigation — reaching a destination, where walking around may be the best answer — is a different task and never shares a success label with it. Rates are over all assigned trials, so a rule that rejects every candidate scores zero completions rather than looking perfect.

What the mid-walk prompt switch does, and what we withdrew Switching the prompt from walking to stepping at frame 52 produced the step in 3 of 8 fresh seeds where an earlier run of the same arm produced none: pooled, 3 of 16 against 6 of 8 when the prompt is given from the start. The step arrives 0.8–3.0 s after the switch and none of those clips clears a box at the position predicted for the switch. On that evidence we withdrew our earlier claims that a later prompt does not elicit the step and that the step is tied to the start of the rollout; what survives is that a delayed prompt elicits it less often, later, and not at the specified place. The handoff does transmit — the delayed prompt moves the joints after the switch by 0.14–0.20 rad RMS, while the frames before the switch are bit-for-bit identical to the walking arm.
Question 2

Could a selector have found it?

Choosing a motion the controller can execute is not the same as choosing a motion that solves the scene task. So we tracked all 64 references — the ones our screen flags and the ones it passes alike — and asked how many satisfy both requirements at once.

The two useful sets are disjoint. Every reference that clears the box was stopped by the evaluator, and every rollout that completed came from a reference that does not clear the box. No selection rule over this pool could have succeeded — including an offline oracle with access to every outcome — at any candidate budget up to the whole pool. For this stepping family the limitation is the candidate pool, not the choice among candidates, and a better verifier would not change the outcome.

Why coverage and selection are reported separately Pool coverage — does any candidate complete the task — and selected success — does the deployed rule pick one that does — answer different questions. High coverage with low selected success is a selector problem. Low coverage at every budget is a candidate problem no ranking function can fix. Clearing references that fail in execution are a controller-compatibility problem. Reporting them apart says which problem you actually have before investing in a better selector.

The same rollouts, scored against the whole traversal problem

A single "clearance preserved" number merges a robot that drove its body through the box with one that never left the first metre. So every rollout is scored against the scene as a task — start at the origin, goal 7.2 m away, box at 1.2 m — and given one outcome.

It is a statement about the achieved motion, not about contact dynamics.

Question 3

Can the loss be predicted before a rollout?

The controller's evaluator stopped 53 of the 64 rollouts on its tracking-error rule. All 53 were upright at the last archived state — pelvis between 0.56 m and 0.95 m, none below 0.5 m — so the cutoff is a rule firing, not an observed fall.

The screen at its calibrated threshold

A threshold of 0.20 s, calibrated on tracker-successful references before any of these clips existed, rejects every stopped reference and passes 8 of the 11 completed runs — rejecting three of them. A post hoc sweep optimum at 0.32 s gives 51 of 53 and 11 of 11 and is reported as a sweep, never as the rule.

Cutoffs landing at the unsupported period

The cutoff falls within 0.2 s of the start of that period in 47 of the 53 stopped rollouts, median +0.04 s. At the cutoff the robot is upright while the reference's higher foot is a median 0.40 m in the air.

0 / 44

Produced steps that pass the screen

None of the 44 references that produce the step passes the screen; the 8 that pass it are clips that did not step, and all 8 completed tracking. Selecting a motion the controller can execute and selecting a motion that solves the scene task pull in opposite directions here, and the conflict has to be named rather than averaged away.

A foot meets the support test at a frame when its sole is within 4.65 cm of the ground and its planar speed is below 1.18 m/s. The screen predicts one controller evaluator's stopping rule under the release configuration. It does not establish zero contact force, a fall, or physical impossibility, and a calibrated score is not a physical guarantee. The panel figure is on the project page.

The prospective test ranks cutoffs but fails admission

128 fresh references — 32 seeds under each of four native root settings — were generated and scored, and the screen's per-clip predictions were committed to the repository before any tracking run: 95 flagged, 33 passed. All 128 were then tracked under the release evaluator. Prospective AUC is 0.855 (bootstrap 95 % 0.742–0.943), below the preregistered 0.90 bar. Of the 95 flagged references, 92 terminated; importantly, 20 of the 33 passing references also terminated. The feature therefore carries ranking information but is not a validated execution-admission rule.

Does it generalise?

The same screen ranks ducking cutoffs, weakly

The screen was built on motions that leave the ground, so it could simply be detecting stepping. We tested it on a second behaviour family from a different pipeline: 526 ducking references produced by a planner-and-correction loop, of which 344 were stopped by the same controller's evaluator, across 36 beam scenes. Groups, primaries, direction and the decision rule were fixed before any feature was computed; the outcome labels were already public, so this is post hoc on a completed campaign.

Above chance in every stratum

The unsupported-period feature is the only one of the three above 0.5 in all seven preregistered strata — three crouch-depth bins and four beam counts — from 0.578 to 0.707. Its margin over speed is +0.233 (0.120–0.362) pooled and +0.092 (−0.018–0.210) within scene, so the ordering is established pooled and only suggested once the between-scene confound is removed.

The confound is refuted, not assumed away

That campaign capped clips at 14 s, which made every reference fast, so speed was the obvious alternative explanation. It fails: speed is at or below chance in five of seven strata, and the references that survived are faster than the ones that were stopped (median peak root speed 1.87 vs 1.75 m/s). The two features are near independent, correlation −0.18.

Crouch depth mostly reads the scene

It is the best pooled predictor at 0.707 but falls to 0.565 within scene: deeper crouches were requested in harder scenes, so the pooled number partly reads the scene rather than the reference.

0.297

Not a ducking accept/reject rule

At the calibrated 0.20 s threshold the screen flags 313 of 344 stopped references but also 128 of 182 completed ones, and 441 of the 526 references — 84 % — have an unsupported period longer than 0.20 s. The correction pipeline produces motions without conventional foot support too. Nothing here says a ducking reference that passes the screen is trackable.

Post hoc, one physics seed, one rollout per reference, one controller checkpoint, 36 scenes of one beam family; intervals are a cluster bootstrap over scenes. What this adds is that the screen is a property of this controller's evaluator applied to references rather than of stepping — two actuation channels in one family plus one different family, all ranking the same way, and far weaker here than on the prompt-elicited stepping references.

Question 4

Does correcting a measured clearance error create a usable candidate?

Ducking admits a generator-level scalar correction: its clearance error is monotone in a control the model reads, so a measured error identifies a candidate fix. Stepping now has a separate reference-level operator: obstacle-relative, support-screened leg IK. Neither has yet produced an obstacle-present traversal.

Measured correction improves reference clearance for the learned proposer — 72.9 % to 99.3 % collision-free — but does not outperform resampling for every proposer: for the heuristic proposer, whose references are already almost always collision-free, resampling reaches the higher margin rate (72.6 % against 64.6 %). The learned proposer's 99.3 % collision-free rate is not its margin rate of 37.5 %. An equal maximum of three generations is not equal computation: 2.72 generations were used under correction against 2.99 under resampling. These are descriptive paired differences over the same rows, not a new experiment.

The boundary All of this is reference geometry. Whether a corrected reference completes the traversal is unmeasured, and a deeper crouch could improve geometry while making tracking harder — a loss we do not yet have the result for. Whether the gain comes specifically from measurement is also untested: a fixed extra-crouch adjustment under the same budget is the mechanism test.

The stepping repair now reaches the execution gate

Over the complete 64-reference source pool, 56 references are refused because the frozen support screen already fails. Of the eight remaining inputs, six fail a post-projection collision, support, residual or edit-budget gate. Two—s4408 and s4434—are admitted for the paired SONIC experiment. This is a severe bottleneck, but it is also a concrete intervention whose next endpoint is closed-loop execution rather than another verifier score.

Process

How the work is run

Every campaign is preregistered before the first sample with its arms, seeds, gates, budget and kill conditions, and stops fail-closed on its own gates. A gate outcome is never rerun on the same seeds with a looser gate: it is diagnosed, the refusal is kept as evidence, and any successor draws seeds it has never seen.

Every clip is archived, including the ones that fail a gate, so a rejected candidate stays replayable.
Scope

What this does not show

The older stepping replay panels mostly omit the obstacle from physics. Except where a panel says otherwise, a reference was tracked on open ground and the recorded robot states were checked afterwards against the box's geometry, so collision is a property of the recorded motion rather than an observed contact. That proxy has since been tested against the real thing. With the box physically present in the simulation, the same 64 references complete local traversal 0 of 64 times at 5 cm and 0 of 64 at 20 cm, against 1 of 64 with no box at all; per reference, the replayed and the felt outcome agree on 63 of 64 at 5 cm. The outcome label remains a geometry check on archived achieved states, not a tracker contact-sensor label. The proxy is faithful, and what it faithfully reports is failure.

The prospective screen operating contract failed. All 128 precommitted predictions now have tracking outcomes: AUC 0.855, 92 / 95 flagged references terminate, and 20 / 33 passing references also terminate. The feature is useful for ranking and diagnosis, not admission. Rollouts without the cutoff rule are still needed to distinguish an evaluator cutoff from a loss of balance.

Two generators scored, one generator executed. ARDY-G1 and a second released, non-autoregressive G1 prior were both tested at the reference-geometry level. Only ARDY-G1 was tracked by the one released SONIC checkpoint. The stepping execution evidence remains one 20 cm-deep box geometry on a straight route, one scene, physics seed 0 and one rollout per reference. Longer history crops, the shorter-horizon checkpoint, re-issued prompts, a second controller and cross-prior execution are untested.

Current ducking passage is not a complete traversal system. A fixed native duck now passes a beam locally in the ASTRA simulation campaign above. The first stepping operator completed 0 / 2 repaired-present local crossings; the older learned duck correction still has reference-level evidence only. Neither that correction nor the proposed progress-aware compiler has a demonstrated fresh-scene execution gain. Contact coverage, motion-quality qualification and downstream utility remain separate unfinished tests.
