Scene2Motion · research project page · updated 2026-09-03

Which generated motions can a controller use to get through this obstacle?

A humanoid may execute a convincing ducking motion and still collide with a low beam if it stands up before reaching the obstacle. Obstacle traversal takes more than a plausible motion or accurate tracking: the robot must produce enough clearance at the obstacle's location and continue to the other side. Scene2Motion studies this gap by separating reference clearance, controller execution and traversal completion, using a pretrained motion model (NVIDIA ARDY-G1) and an existing controller (SONIC) on the Unitree G1, both frozen.

Three motions can all look relevant to "step over an obstacle": one that steps in the wrong place, one that clears the box in the reference but cannot be tracked, and one that clears it and is tracked to the other side. Only the third completes the traversal. The sections below measure how many of each we actually get.

Question 1

Does the generated pool contain a motion for the specified obstacle?

We measure clearance at the position the scene specifies, not anywhere along the route, because a motion can clear the same obstacle somewhere else on its path. We asked the model for a step in every way its released interface allows: the text prompt, a foot-position request, a rotation request aligned to the gait, and a prompt switched on mid-walk.

44 / 64references clear a 3 cm box at some tested position along the route (whole-body geometry check)
12 / 64clear a 5 cm box at the position the scene specifies (1.2 m; chosen after inspecting the clips, now fixed for replication)
3 / 16produce the step when the prompt is switched on mid-walk, 0.8–3.0 s late, none at the predicted position
  • The motion arrives early and on its own schedule. Among the 49 references with positive clearance somewhere on the route, the reference root reaches the location of maximum box clearance after a median 1.4 s, and 40 of 49 within the first two seconds. Where the box is does not move it.
  • Joint-level requests do not place it either. A requested foot lift over-responds by 1.7–2.3× and lifts both feet; a rotation request produces a step 3.2 m from its anchor at half the amplitude; a route stretched to line the step up with the box preserves the intended gait in 1 of 6.
  • No position on the route is much better. Re-scoring all 64 clips against a box at every 5 cm step from 0.6 m to 6.6 m, the best position for a 5 cm box is 1.2 m, where 12 of 64 clear; 8 cm peaks there too at 11 of 64, and 3 cm peaks at 1.15 m with 15 of 64. At the 3.6 m control position it is 2 of 64 at every height. The 1.2 m position was chosen on these same clips and is now fixed for replication.
  • A tolerance window is a different measurement. Allowing the box to move after seeing each clip answers "could this motion have cleared something nearby", not "did it clear the specified obstacle". We keep the two apart and never report a tolerance-window rate as a fixed-obstacle success rate.

What the mid-walk prompt switch does, and what we withdrew

Switching the prompt from walking to stepping at frame 52, through the released minimum-history interface, produced the step in 3 of 8 fresh seeds where an earlier run of the same arm produced none: pooled, 3 of 16 against 6 of 8 when the prompt is given from the start. The step arrives 0.8–3.0 s after the switch and none of those clips clears a box at the position predicted for the switch. On that evidence we withdrew our earlier claims that a later prompt does not elicit the step and that the step is tied to the start of the rollout; the statement that survives is that a delayed prompt elicits it less often, later, and not at the specified place. The handoff does transmit: the delayed prompt moves the joints after the switch by 0.14–0.20 rad RMS, while the frames before the switch are bit-for-bit identical to the walking arm.

A positive control that we refused rather than reinterpreted. The same campaign was designed around a sideways-squeeze prompt as a positive control, and its substrate gate failed: under a dense route constraint the squeeze prompt never produced a sidestep (0 of 8 by the preregistered composite of heading deviation, lateral excursion and foot crossing). It produced a lifting gait instead — 5 of 8 seeds lift, 2 of 8 clear a 5 cm box at the predicted position. The control is inconclusive for the question it was built to answer and has to be re-designed on fresh seeds; we did not relax the gate to rescue it.

Question 2

Could a selector have found it?

Choosing a motion the robot can execute is not the same as choosing a motion that solves the obstacle task. Recent work shows that filtering candidates by predicted execution quality raises tracking success with both generator and controller frozen. So we tracked all 64 references, the ones our screen flags and the ones it passes alike, and asked how many satisfy both requirements at once.

The two useful sets are disjoint. Every reference that clears the box was stopped by the evaluator, and every rollout that completed came from a reference that does not clear the box. No selection rule over this pool could have succeeded, including an offline oracle with access to every outcome, and the result stays zero at every candidate budget up to the whole pool. Sampling more references raises reference clearance and does not move the joint count at all. For this stepping family the limitation is the candidate pool, not the choice among candidates, and a better verifier would not change the outcome.

Why report it this way. Pool coverage (does any candidate complete the task) and selected success (does the deployed rule pick one that does) answer different questions. High coverage with low selected success is a selector problem. Low coverage at every budget is a candidate problem no ranking function can fix. Clearing references that fail in execution are a controller-compatibility problem. Reporting them separately says which problem you actually have before you invest in a better selector.

Question 3

Why does tracking lose the clearance, and can it be predicted before a rollout?

The controller's evaluator stopped 53 of the 64 rollouts on its tracking-error rule. All 53 were upright at the last archived state, so the cutoff is a rule firing, not an observed fall.

Definition. Clearance is preserved when a reference that clears the obstacle yields a tracked trajectory that passes through the obstacle corridor, finishes beyond the obstacle, remains collision-free, and satisfies the stated termination rule. A geometry query alone is not enough: a robot that stops before the box looks collision-free. An unguarded query reports 43 of 64 achieved clips clearing the 5 cm box; the guarded endpoint reports zero, and the difference is robots that never arrived.
0 / 64references whose clearance is preserved after tracking, and still zero with the termination condition dropped
53 / 64rollouts stopped by the evaluator's tracking-error cutoff; all 53 upright at the last archived state (pelvis 0.56–0.95 m)
53 / 53 · 8 / 11the reference screen rejects every stopped reference and passes 8 of the 11 completed runs, rejecting three

The outcome, scored against the whole traversal problem

A boolean "clearance preserved" merges two very different failures: a robot that drove its body through the box, and one that never left the first metre. So we score every rollout against the scene as a task — start at the origin, goal 7.2 m away, box at 1.2 m — and give it one outcome.

The pool fails by colliding, not only by being stopped. At the 5 cm box, 21 of the 64 rollouts drive the body through the obstacle's volume and 43 are stopped by the evaluator before anything else happens. Nothing falls, nothing stalls, nothing runs out of time, and nothing completes the traversal at any height. Completion requires passing the obstacle inside the corridor: walking around it is not a completed local traversal. This is strictly more informative than a single "zero preserved", which merges a robot that drove its body through the box with one that never left the first metre.

The 21 collisions include every one of the 11 rollouts that were never cut off: all 11 reach and pass the obstacle's position, and all 11 intersect the box there. The other 10 were also stopped by the evaluator, later than the intersection, which is why collision outranks the cutoff in the outcome ordering. Fifty rollouts never reach the obstacle at all. Raising the box from 3 cm to 30 cm moves collisions from 21 to 31 and cutoffs from 43 to 33, and leaves completions at zero throughout.

All 64 assigned trials were executed; none was rejected before execution, and rates are over all 64. These rollouts were tracked with the box absent from the physics scene, so "hit the box" means the recorded motion intersects its volume in replay — the robot never felt contact and its controller was never perturbed by one. It is a statement about the achieved motion, not about contact dynamics. Putting the obstacle in the simulation is the next experiment; obstacle-present traversal has never been measured in this project, for either behaviour family.

What the screen measures

A foot meets the support test when its sole is within 4.65 cm of the ground and moving slower than 1.18 m/s. Long periods in which neither foot meets that test are associated with the tracking-error cutoffs: in all 12 clearing references both feet fail it for 0.44–1.04 s (nine clips) or 2.4–3.1 s (three) while the pelvis rises only 2–26 cm, and the evaluator's cutoff lands within ±0.2 s of the start of that period in 47 of 53 rollouts. A simple flight-time comparison (the period is 1.3–15.6× longer than a ballistic flight for that pelvis rise) motivates further physical validation; it is not a dynamics analysis, and failing the support test does not establish zero contact force.

What the screen does and does not say. It predicts one evaluator's stopping rule under the release configuration. It does not say the robot would fall, and it does not say the motion is physically impossible. A calibrated score is not a physical guarantee. We evaluate the screen and the ducking correction as separate components, and to evaluate the screen we track both flagged and passed references.

Prospective test, in progress

128 fresh references (32 seeds under four root-control settings) were generated, scored, and the screen's per-clip predictions committed to the repository before any tracking run — 95 flagged, 33 passed, file hash recorded in the campaign receipt. All four settings are constructible by their preregistered criteria, so the comparison is between four requests the model actually honours. The free setting replicates the original prospectively (23 of 32 produce the step against 44 of 64 before; 7 of 32 clear at the specified position against 12 of 64). The tracking stage has not run: outcomes and clearance after tracking are pending for all 128 references, flagged and passed alike, as are the rollouts without the cutoff rule that would say what the physical outcome is.

"Valid step passing the support test" requires a locally valid step and no unsupported period longer than 0.20 s, which is why it is zero while 33 references pass the screen alone. Pinning the root shortens the unsupported period (median 0.48 s free, 0.22 s with both pinned) and lowers the peak pelvis height from 0.955 m to 0.77 m, but it does not turn the motion into a supported step: under all four native root settings the prompt puts its lift inside a period with neither foot supported. A prescriptive contract of the form "produce a valid supported step that clears the box" therefore has nothing to select before tracking even begins.

Does the screen generalise?

The same screen ranks ducking cutoffs, weakly — and it is not a stepping artefact

The screen was built on motions that leave the ground, so it could simply be detecting stepping. We tested it on a second behaviour family produced by a different pipeline: 526 ducking references from a planner-and-correction loop, of which 344 were stopped by the same controller's evaluator, across 36 beam scenes. The groups, the primary feature of each, the direction and the decision rule were fixed before any feature was computed; the outcome labels were already public, so this is a post hoc test on a completed campaign.

0.674ranking performance (AUC) of the unsupported-period feature on 526 ducking references, 95 % interval 0.625–0.721 over 36 scenes; 0.694 within scene
0.441the speed confound the plan named in advance, interval 0.328–0.552 spanning chance: reference speed does not predict ducking cutoffs
0.297specificity at the calibrated 0.20 s threshold: as an accept/reject rule for ducking it would reject most of the corpus
  • Above chance in every stratum. The unsupported-period feature is the only one of the three above 0.5 in all seven preregistered strata (three crouch-depth bins, four beam counts), from 0.578 to 0.707.
  • The confound is refuted, not assumed away. The earlier ducking campaign capped clips at 14 s, which forced every reference to be fast, so speed was the obvious alternative explanation. It fails: speed is at or below chance in five of the seven strata, and the references that survived are faster than the ones that were stopped (median peak root speed 1.87 vs 1.75 m/s). The two features are near-independent, correlation −0.18, so the screen is not a repackaged speed measure.
  • Crouch depth mostly reads the scene, not the reference. It is the best pooled predictor at 0.707, but falls to 0.565 within scene: deeper crouches were requested in harder scenes.
  • What it does not license. The transfer is directional and far weaker than on the stepping family, and the screen is not usable as a ducking accept/reject rule: at its calibrated threshold it flags 313 of 344 stopped references but also 128 of 182 completed ones, and 441 of the 526 references (84 %) have an unsupported period longer than 0.20 s. The correction pipeline produces motions without conventional foot support too. Nothing here says a ducking reference that passes the screen is trackable.

Post hoc on a completed campaign, one physics seed, one rollout per reference, one controller checkpoint, 36 scenes of one beam family; intervals are a cluster bootstrap over scenes. What this adds is that the screen is a property of this controller's evaluator applied to references rather than of stepping: two actuation channels in one family plus one different family, all ranking the same way.

Question 4

Where selection has nothing to select, does correction create a usable candidate?

Ducking references admit a correction because their clearance error is scalar and monotone: the model's root-height control moves overhead clearance in a predictable direction, so a measured error identifies the fix. Δq = e / |g′|, applied at most twice, remeasured after each step, rejected with its residual error if the response saturates. Stepping has no such control in our experiments and is not corrected.

The supported conclusion. Measured correction improves reference clearance for the learned proposer, but does not outperform resampling for every proposer: the benefit depends on the starting planner. The learned proposer's 99.3 % collision-free rate is not its margin rate of 37.5 %. For the heuristic proposer, whose references are already almost always collision-free, resampling reaches the higher margin rate. The paired differences are a descriptive reanalysis of the same rows, resampling the 36 scenes, not a new experiment. An equal maximum of three generations is not equal computation, as the generations column shows. Whether the gain comes specifically from measurement is untested: a fixed extra-crouch adjustment under the same budget is the mechanism test.

All of this is reference geometry. Whether a corrected reference completes the traversal is unmeasured. A deeper crouch could improve geometry while making tracking harder, and that possible loss is a result we do not yet have. An earlier tracking study of 526 ducking references preserved clearance in none, under a protocol whose 14 s clip cap stopped most rollouts before the beam; it says nothing about the corrections either way.

What we keep

Motion records with rejection reasons

Every candidate is kept, accepted or not, with its measurements and the reason it was rejected. A rejected candidate is a statement about that reference under this controller's evaluator; it does not mean the scene cannot be traversed.

What a record carries

Scene and obstacle; the request (prompt, seed, controls); where the motion occurred; clearance at the specified position; the support-test result and the screen's prediction; correction steps and residual error; the evaluator outcome and cutoff time; clearance after tracking; physics seed; provenance for the model, runtime, robot model and controller.

Rejection labels

Reference intersects the obstacle · screen predicts an evaluator cutoff · tracking stops before reaching the obstacle · achieved motion clears the obstacle. Four separate labels, not one "untraversable".

Rates are reported over all assigned trials. A rule that rejects a candidate without executing it scores that trial as a non-completion; where rejection avoided an unsafe attempt we report that separately. Rejecting everything must never read as perfect task performance. Ducking pilot, 300 procedural scenes: 268 collision-free references (192 meeting the 18 cm margin, 76 below it), 6 rejected after correction with their residual error, 26 refused before correction with a reason. Stepping: the 64 references carry every label; the 128 fresh ones carry the reference-side labels and the screen's prediction, with tracking outcome pending.

What comes next

Two experiments decide what this becomes

The evidence so far is an evaluation contribution: where generation, selection and tracking fail for obstacle-relative tasks. It becomes a method contribution only if a mechanism is shown to improve actual traversal. We do not promise the second before measuring it.

1 · Complete the prospective screen evaluation

The rule is frozen and the per-clip predictions are committed. What is missing is the tracking itself: outcomes for all 128 fresh references, flagged and passed; the original 64 re-run without the tracking-error cutoff, so the physical outcome can be classified as fell, stalled, continued or cleared instead of inferred from a rule firing; and known-trackable walking and jumping controls, so the screen is tested against legitimate dynamic motion rather than only against motion without conventional foot support. The jump reference ships with the controller checkout but in its deployment format, so it needs a duration- and ordering-preserving conversion first.

2 · A shared-pool obstacle-position study, beam present

Hold the instruction, start state, controller and candidate pool fixed; move the beam to positions fixed in advance; compare random selection, a trackability-and-text rule, geometry-only selection, the combination, and an offline oracle. Report pool coverage separately from selected success, and compare extra sampling, a fixed extra-crouch adjustment and the measured correction under one budget. This needs the beam spawned in the physics scene as a contact-sensing body, an environment spacing and episode length that let a 7.2 m route reach it — the shipped defaults are 2.0 m and 10 s — and an evaluator validated on a known-trackable positive control. Only then does "hit the obstacle" become an observed contact rather than a geometry replay.

Local traversal, which this project measures, means passing through a specified corridor; walking around does not count. Navigation, which it does not measure, means reaching a destination, where walking around may be the best answer, and adds route choice, transitions, replanning and recovery. Whether records with rejection reasons help a navigation policy is a separate question. Protocols, seeds, schedules and the full history are in the research log, the EXP-029 protocol and the plan of record.
