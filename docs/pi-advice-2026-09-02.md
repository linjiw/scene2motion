I would keep **Scene2Motion** as the project name, but organize the paper around a simpler question:

> **Can a humanoid produce the right motion, at the right place, and still clear the obstacle when a controller follows it?**

I read your draft, the project page, and the repository's current framing, experiment protocols, and selected result records. I checked the headline repair counts, but did not rerun the experiments.

## 1. What to learn from Xiao's writing here

*Moving Through Clutter* is especially relevant. It starts with a concrete mismatch: impressive humanoid motion in open spaces does not automatically solve movement through clutter. It then connects the scene geometry, motion data, and evaluation, while acknowledging limitations in retargeting and tracking.

For your paper, I would borrow three principles from that argument structure:

- **Start with the obstacle, not the software pipeline.** The robot must duck under a beam or step over a box.
- **Explain the missing capability.** Producing a stepping motion does not ensure that it happens at the box.
- **Separate evidence levels.** A collision-free reference, a completed tracking run, and successful obstacle traversal are different results.

That gives your project a clear scientific identity: **testing the gap between generated motion and usable robot motion, with targeted repairs where measurement helps.**

## 2. Recommended title and project goal

### Recommended paper title

**Scene2Motion: Evaluating Generated Humanoid Motions for Obstacle Traversal**

A shorter, question-led alternative:

**Can Generated Humanoid Motions Clear Obstacles?**

I prefer the first for the manuscript. It names the application and matches the evidence without implying that you have already built a successful navigation system.

"Contract Before Control" could remain a presentation subtitle, but I would not make *contract* the main scientific concept. Your current screen predicts a particular evaluator's stopping behavior; the word can suggest a stronger guarantee.

### Plain-language project goal

> Turn generated humanoid motions into useful candidates for moving through clutter: check whether they fit the scene, improve their clearance when possible, and test whether a controller can reproduce them.

### Research goal

> Determine when a pretrained humanoid motion model can provide useful references for obstacle traversal without retraining the model or controller. Measure whether scene placement and clearance survive tracking, and evaluate whether feedback-based correction and reference screening improve outcomes under a fixed sampling budget.

The longer-term navigation goal—choosing whether to duck, step over, or take another route—belongs in the roadmap until that complete behavior has been demonstrated.

## 3. Rewritten abstract

This version reflects the current evidence in the tracking results (`docs/ramp-exp022-exact-tracking-result-2026-09-01.md`) and repair experiment records (`outputs/phase4e_architecture_v2_s8/experiment.json`).

> Generating a humanoid motion is not enough to traverse an obstacle: the motion must occur at the right place and remain collision-free when tracked. We present Scene2Motion, a framework for evaluating and repairing generated motions while keeping the motion model and controller fixed. Using ARDY-G1 and SONIC in simulation, we separate motion generation, obstacle-relative clearance, and clearance after tracking. Of 64 step-prompted references, 12 cleared a 5 cm box at a position selected after inspecting the motions; none retained clearance after tracking. Tracking ran without the obstacle, with recorded robot states subsequently checked against its geometry. A foot-support screen flagged all 53 references whose rollouts triggered the configured tracking-error cutoffs, while also rejecting three of the eleven runs that did not terminate early. This analysis predicts evaluator behavior, not whether the robot would fall. For ducking motions, we use measured clearance errors to adjust the generation controls. Across 36 beam scenes and eight seeds, up to two repairs increased collision-free references from 72.9% to 99.3% for a learned proposal model, compared with 77.4% for resampling under the same three-attempt limit. These improvements concern reference geometry; successful obstacle traversal has not yet been demonstrated under the tested tracking protocols. The results identify limits of the current generator–controller combination and show where measured feedback improves clearance. Prospective screen validation and obstacle-present execution remain future work.

I deliberately left dataset throughput out of the abstract. It distracts from the main finding, and generation speed is not yet the rate of producing successfully executed traversal examples.

## 4. How I would guide the next project work

### First: establish what the screen actually predicts

Complete the already-planned fresh-reference test and simulation runs without tracking-error cutoffs. These answer different questions:

- Does the screen predict early termination on new references?
- Without that termination rule, does the robot fall, stall, or continue?
- Does the achieved motion clear the obstacle geometry?

Your EXP-024 results and EXP-028 protocol already recognize this distinction.

Include known-trackable controls, including an appropriate jumping example, before interpreting an airborne phase as evidence of physical impossibility.

### Second: complete one convincing ducking experiment

Ducking is the clearest place to connect your repair mechanism to an executed benefit.

Start with a walking control that reaches the obstacle location. Then compare uncorrected motions, repaired motions, and resampling across predetermined beam heights, with sufficient rollout duration and the beam present in simulation.

The question is:

> **Does correcting the reference's clearance also improve the robot's achieved clearance?**

Keep collisions, failure to reach the beam, and evaluator cutoffs separate.

Also preserve the losing comparisons. Your repair helps the learned proposer substantially, but the heuristic proposer's resampling baseline achieves a higher clearance-margin success rate than repair. Moreover, the learned proposer's **99.3% collision-free rate is not its 18 cm margin success rate**, which is 37.5%. Those distinctions make the result more informative.

### Third: make rejection labels useful without treating them as ground truth

A rejected candidate does not necessarily mean the scene is physically impossible to traverse.

Record labels such as:

- Reference intersects the obstacle.
- Screen predicts an evaluator cutoff.
- Tracking stops before reaching the obstacle.
- Achieved motion clears the obstacle.

That is more useful for downstream learning than a single "untraversable" label. Whether these records improve a navigation policy remains a separate experiment.

One small but important table correction: EXP-024's "contact-consistent" column means **a valid local step that also passes the support test**, not merely passing the screen. Label it **"Valid step passing the support test"** so the zero counts do not appear to contradict the 33 screen-passing references.

### Suggested project-page opening

> A humanoid can generate a stepping motion without stepping over the box in front of it. Scene2Motion studies this gap between motion generation and obstacle traversal. Using a pretrained motion model and an existing controller, we test where motions occur, whether they clear the scene, and whether clearance survives tracking. We also use measured clearance errors to improve ducking motions and record why other candidates are rejected.

I would lead with your same-motion, different-obstacle comparison, then organize the page around **placement, tracking, and repair**. Keep experiment identifiers, implementation history, and scheduling details in the linked research log.

The central writing lesson is: **make the reader understand the robot's problem before asking them to understand your terminology.**
